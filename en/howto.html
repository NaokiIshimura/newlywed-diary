How to play

Newlywed Diary is a life simulation that follows a married couple's household finances one year at a time, from the wedding to retirement at 65. The only decisions you make are whether to buy something or skip it, and whether to go High, Mid or Low. This page explains everything from the first setup to how each year plays out, the life events and random events, how the money is calculated, and the results at retirement. The English version starts in the United States, with amounts in US dollars, and you can switch to Singapore (amounts in Singapore dollars), the Philippines (amounts in Philippine pesos), Canada (amounts in Canadian dollars) or Australia (amounts in Australian dollars) under "Country or region" in the setup. The numbers on this page are for the United States; the sections near the end cover the other countries.

What you decide at the start

  • Country or region: the United States, Singapore, the Philippines, Canada or Australia. Incomes, prices and currency follow the country you choose.
  • Your gender and your partner's gender: choose "Female" or "Male" for each. Your partner is the one whose work style can change.
  • Age at marriage: 22 to 45. The younger you start, the more years you work.
  • Savings: $0 to $150K, entered in thousands of dollars.
  • Your incomes: the gross annual income for you and for your partner (if working full-time), entered in thousands of dollars. The defaults are the median income for your age at marriage. If you change the age, any income you haven't edited by hand is updated to the median for the new age.
  • Work style: choose "Dual income (full-time)", "Dual income (part-time)" or "Single income". Part-time halves your partner's income, and single income sets it to 0.

Every item has a "Random" button, and "Randomize all and start" picks everything at random and starts the game right away.

How a year goes by

Once the game starts, it moves forward one year at a time on its own. You can switch the pace with "1x", "2x" and "Pause". When a life event or an event with a choice comes up, the game stops and opens a choice screen. After you choose, the years start moving again.

At the end of each year, that year's income and spending are calculated, your savings go up or down, and a "Year end" line is written in the diary. A breakdown of the year-end finances appears at the bottom of the screen for a few seconds (you can turn it off with "Show year-end finances" in Settings).

The status bar at the top of the screen shows your age, how many years you've been married, savings, work style, your kids' ages, car and home. Savings are shown in red when they are negative.

How choices work

Most events are decided in two steps. First you choose whether to buy (or do) it or skip it, and only if you buy do you choose High, Mid or Low. Each button shows the cost and what your savings will be after you choose it ("→ Savings $…").

  • Not now: for a car, a home and children, you'll be asked again in 3 years.
  • Never: for a car ("Never own one"), a home ("Rent for life") and children ("Never"), choosing this means you'll never be asked again.

For a home, you pay 20% of the price as a down payment (or as much as your savings allow if they fall short), and repay the rest each year on a 30-year mortgage at 6.5% interest. Owning a home adds property tax, homeowners insurance and money set aside for upkeep every year (plus HOA fees for an existing house). Until you buy a home, rent for a family-size place costs $20K a year.

How the money is calculated each year

Income is your annual income plus your partner's (multiplied by the share for their work style), multiplied by an age-based growth factor and by the take-home rate (75%, after income taxes, payroll taxes and health insurance premiums). The growth factor compares the median income for your current age with the median for your age at marriage; it peaks in your 40s and eases off slightly from your mid-50s. On top of this come the Child Tax Credit ($2,200 a year per child under 17) and any money sent home by children who have moved out.

Example: if you marry at 28 and both work full-time earning $57K each, your take-home income in year 1 is ($57K + $57K) × 75% = $85.5K.

Spending is the total of the following:

  • The couple's basic living costs: starting at $35K a year and rising 1.25% every year
  • Kids' living costs: $5K a year for ages 0 to 5, $7K for elementary school, $8.5K for junior high, $9.5K for high school, and $6K a year while attending college from home
  • Education: the yearly cost of the childcare and schools you chose, through university
  • Housing: rent until you own a home ($17K a year as newlyweds, $20K a year for a family-size rental), then the fixed costs and mortgage payments once you own one
  • Car: upkeep and repair bills for breakdowns. Without a car, buses and rideshares cost $6K a year while you have children at home
  • Parent care, a child's student loan you've taken over, and interest on debt (if any)

Random events

Each year there's a 30% chance that one of the following happens. This is why the same choices can lead to a different life every time you play.

  • Promotion: your or your partner's income rises 10%
  • Job offer: if you choose "Switch jobs", your income changes by anywhere from −20% to +20%. If you "Decline", it stays the same
  • Illness or injury: out-of-pocket medical bills of $2K–$9K
  • Parent care (from age 40, up to twice): for 3 to 5 years, your share of a "Care facility" costs $10K a year. "Care at home" costs only $3.5K a year, but your partner switches to part-time (half the income) while caregiving
  • Bonus cut: take-home pay is down 10% that year
  • Windfall: $5K–$80K from the lottery or an inheritance

Separately, the car and home you own carry their own risks. A used car breaks down with a 35% chance each year (5% for a new or luxury car), and repairs cost $2K–$6K. Home repairs come up each year with a 40% chance for a fixer-upper (5% for an existing house, 3% for a large new house), and you choose how to fix it: "Full renovation", "Partial repair" or "Quick fix". The more expensive the fix, the longer it lasts until the next repair (15, 8 or 3 years). Repairs on a fixer-upper cost twice as much, and 12 years after you buy it, a major renovation costs $80K.

Work style and kids moving out

If your partner left work under single income and then goes back, their income is 4% lower for each year they were away (up to 40%).

When a child moves out, they may send money home. Your education points (0 to 10) count your choices from childcare through university: 2 points for "High", 1 for "Mid" and 0 for "Low". The higher the points, the higher the chance of getting money (20% plus 5% per point, and another +20% for a child who got a job after high school) and the larger the amount ($1K–$5K a year). In years when the household can't fully cover university tuition, the child may take out a student loan, and when they move out, you as parents may take it over and repay it over 10 years (this is less likely with more education points).

If your savings go negative

When your savings fall below 0, the shortfall is treated as debt and charged 15% interest every year. If your savings drop below −$150K, you get the "Bankrupt Ending" and the game ends there.

Retirement and results

When you retire at 65, your rank is decided by your net worth (savings + the value of your home and car − the mortgage left).

The results screen shows a title based on how you tended to choose (9 kinds), the achievements you earned (12 in all), a breakdown of your lifetime spending, and a life timeline. It also shows the difference compared with picking "Mid" for everything under the same starting conditions. You can turn your result into an image to save or share, or "Post to X (Twitter)". The endings, titles and achievements you've seen can be checked anytime under "Endings" on the title screen.

Saving your progress

Your progress is saved automatically in your browser every time a year passes and every time you make a choice. Even if you close the page partway through, you can pick up where you left off with "Continue" on the title screen the next time you open it. You can also keep playing after switching languages. Saves are kept per browser, so they don't carry over to other devices or browsers. "Delete data" in Settings erases your save in progress, endings, achievements and settings.

Tips for doing well

  • Choosing "Low" for everything isn't always best. When used-car breakdowns and fixer-upper repairs pile up, cheap choices can end up costing more
  • Save the "High" choices for things that pay off over the long run. Simply keeping both partners working full-time makes a big difference to your income
  • The most expensive stretch is when your kids are at university. If your children are close in age, tuition bills overlap, so build up your savings before then
  • Watch the "→ Savings" amount on each button, and look for choices that keep your savings from going negative

The amounts in the game are simplified estimates for gameplay, based on public statistics and typical market prices. They don't include Social Security, retirement accounts, inflation or detailed tax calculations. For real household budgets, mortgages or education savings plans, please consult a professional such as a financial planner.
